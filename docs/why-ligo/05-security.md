# Security considerations

## Better than local passwords

| Question | Local passwords | SPIFFE, SPIRE and `ligo` |
| :---------------------------- | :------------------------------------ | :---------------------------------------------- |
| How does a workload prove who it is? | It knows a secret that anyone could have copied | It holds an SVID, issued after the platform vouched for its machine, and valid for minutes or hours |
| Who has seen the secret? | Everyone who configured, deployed or debugged it | Nobody: the target's credential is generated by a plugin, kept in Vault, and released only to one SPIFFE ID |
| How many applications share it? | Often several | One binding, one principal, one credential |
| What may it do? | Whatever the account was once given | Only the privileges of the approved access level, within the platform's bounds |
| Who agreed to it? | A ticket somewhere, if anyone | Every owner concerned, recorded with the change |
| How often is it changed? | Rarely, because every copy must change | On a schedule, by `ligo`; or never stored at all, with dynamic credentials that live only for a lease |
| How is it taken away? | By hunting for every user of the account | Any owner revokes the binding: the workload stops being served at once, and the role is dropped |
| What does a thief get? | Lasting access, from anywhere | A short-lived SVID, or a credential tied to one binding and soon rotated |

Some targets, such as PostgreSQL today, still check a password at the door, because they know nothing of SPIFFE. The difference is everything around it: no person ever handles that password, it opens one binding's access only, and the workload must prove its identity to Vault every time it reads it.

## Visibility, across every technology

Because every binding goes through `ligo`, its database becomes the inventory that the password model never had: **every workload that may use every target, at which level, approved by whom and when, and through which network flows**. It holds this the same way for a container in the data centre, an OpenStack instance, an EC2 instance in AWS or a pod in GCP, since all of them are named by a SPIFFE ID.

- **For security teams**, the question "who can reach the payments database?" has one answer, from one place, whatever the technology of the callers.
- **For auditors**, every change and approval is in an append-only audit log, kept as a hash chain, so a deleted or altered entry is detected.
- **For network teams**, each target declares the flows needed to reach it, and each workload can read its own, so firewall rules can be checked against what was approved.
- **For incident response**, revoking one binding, or every binding of a compromised source on a target, takes one command, and it takes effect in the API at once.

## What you still have to get right

`ligo` and SPIRE move the trust; they do not remove it. Plan for these:

- **The SPIRE Server is the crown jewel.** Whoever runs it can issue any identity in its trust domain. Protect it as you protect a certificate authority, and separate trust domains where the people must differ.
- **Registration entries decide who is who.** A workload registered under the wrong SPIFFE path gets the wrong access. Base entries on high-trust selectors: those the platform maintains, not those any instance editor can change.
- **`ligo`'s own administrators and its database** are protected by SVID authentication, approvals and the audit trail, but they remain a target worth defending.
- **Current limits.** PostgreSQL is the only platform provider shipped today, and the Vault store releases a credential to one exact SPIFFE ID, so a source must name one workload, not a pattern, on such a platform. The other documents list every limit.
